Rarity, Reasoning, and Retrieval in Multilingual Entity Linking

Popularity is not rarity. Prior work usually defines rare entities using popularity metrics such as pageviews. We show that these definitions miss many entities that are poorly documented or weakly connected. As a result, previous evaluations have underestimated the scope of the rare-entity problem. We then show that reasoning with retrieval helps link these entities.

Parinthapat Pengpun · Simran Khanuja · Graham Neubig

Carnegie Mellon University · NeuLab

The two approaches identify different entities. Their rare sets overlap by only 37% on average, and current systems struggle on the entities that popularity metrics miss.

What helps

A model that reasons while searching Wikipedia improves accuracy most on these rare entities.

Finding 1 · Rarity is multidimensional

Low traffic and low coverage are not the same problem.

An entity can receive public attention and still have little structured or cross-lingual information. A single popularity score cannot separate these cases.

Different metrics, different tails

The average pair shares only 37% of its rare entities.

Some cross-family pairs overlap by only 10%. Popularity-only evaluation therefore leaves many structurally sparse entities untested.

Takeaway. Entity rarity depends on the resource signal used to define it.

The failures are severe

Accuracy falls by 15.4–39.9%.

Pageviews expose a 37.7% drop. Statement count exposes a similar 37.0% drop on a largely different set of entities.

The effect is a gradient

No single cutoff creates the finding.

Accuracy declines smoothly toward the sparse end. The trend also remains stable at 1%, 5%, and 10% thresholds.

Search is part of the reasoning process.

Instead of reranking one fixed candidate list, the model decides what to search, reads the evidence, and can refine its query.

Finding 3 · The controlled interaction

Reasoning and retrieval are complementary.

Reasoning alone does not significantly improve rare-entity accuracy. Retrieval helps rare entities, but without reasoning it can hurt overall accuracy. Together they perform best.

Results · MERLIN across five languages

The advantage grows toward the long tail.

The best system reaches 87.9% average accuracy, +6.9% over the prior state of the art. Fourteen of 15 rare-slice gains are larger than the full-set gain.

Rare-slice advantage

Structural tails produce the largest gains.

The gain reaches +23.3% for qualifiers, +22.1% for statements, and +21.7% for Wikidata outgoing links.

Reasoning or scale?

A reasoning 4B model beats an 8B instruct model on rare entities.

They are tied on the full set. On structural slices, the smaller reasoning model leads by roughly 5–7%.

Robustness

Our system degrades less on 14 of 15 rarity dimensions.

The largest robustness gaps appear on language editions, statement count, qualifiers, and graph links.

Inside the search loop

More searches do not mean better search.

Thinking models search less often, but spend much longer reasoning between calls. Instruct models keep searching even as their queries become repetitive.

Search 2 matters most

Refinement helps. Extra searches quickly stop helping.

The first result informs a stronger second query. Hit rates fall sharply after that point.

Stopping is a capability

Instruct-model queries degrade over long search chains.

Verbatim repetition reaches 34%. Strong reasoning models usually stop after they have enough evidence.

Open artifacts

MERLIN-Rare makes the long tail easy to test.

We release the union of the 15 rare-entity slices with images, metric values, percentiles, and slice flags. The release also includes predictions and lossless traces for every complete paper configuration.
